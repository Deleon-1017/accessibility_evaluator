## Related Literature

This section reviews the conceptual principles, established standards, and documented realities that define the field of web accessibility, providing the essential context for the project.

### Theoretical and Legal Foundations of Web Accessibility

The essentials for web accessibility are embedded in many overlapping frameworks: the societal model of disability, principles of universal design, and human rights law. The societal model puts forward that people are disabled, not only by impairments but also in social barriers, particularly designed digital content. Universal design designated for creating products and environments usable by all people, to the highest extent without the need of adaptation. These frameworks have been structured into legal aspects such as the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) and mandated national laws like Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). Court rulings become more aware for interpreting laws to apply to digital environment, establishing web accessibility not as a best practice but as a civil and a non-negotiable legal requirement for public and commercial unit (Abhichandani, 2025).

### The WCAG 2.1 Standard: Principles, Guidelines, and Conformance

According to W3C (2023), The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.1, developed by World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), evaluates accessibility principles into a technically testable framework. It is formed by fore core principles: Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, and Robust, which breaks down into 13 guidelines and 78 testable success criteria, which assigned each level: A (minimum), AA (standard), or AAA (high level). It is important to learn that not all success criteria are "machine-testable". Criteria requiring assessment on the "meaningfulness" of the text or the logical order of content fall outside the area of pure code analysis.

### The Persistent Implementation Gap: Empirical Evidence and Root Causes

A substantial body of empirical research documents a severe and persistent gap between accessibility standards and real-world implementation. The annual WebAIM Million Report provides the most cited large-scale evidence, with the 2025 analysis finding that 94.8% of the world's top home pages have detectable WCAG failures (WebAIM, 2025). The report identified the same six error categories as most prevalent for the sixth consecutive year, indicating a systemic failure. Beyond prevalence studies, research into root causes points to a multifaceted problem: a lack of developer awareness and training, the perceived complexity of implementation, and its low prioritization in project management (Abhichandani, 2025). This gap between awareness and practical skill is precisely the educational niche this project aims to address.

### Designing Inclusive Web Applications with Accessibility in Mind

Patan (2024) explored the critical importance of designing inclusive web applications with accessibility in mind. It delves into ethical, legal, and business imperatives driving the need for accessible web design, highlighting that over one billion people worldwide live with some form of disability. The article discusses key accessibility standards, particularly the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), and outlines essential techniques and tools for implementing and testing web accessibility. These include semantic HTML, ARIA roles and attributes, color contrast considerations, keyboard navigation, and alternative text for images. The article also examines various testing tools such as Lighthouse, WAVE, axe DevTools, and screen readers. Furthermore, it presents a compelling business case for accessibility, emphasizing benefits such as an expanded user base, improved SEO, enhanced brand reputation, and legal compliance. Through comprehensive exploration of these topics, the article underscores that accessibility is not just a regulatory requirement but a fundamental responsibility that leads to better digital experiences for all users.

### Web Accessibility and User Experience: An Integrated Perspective

Recent research has strengthened the connection between web accessibility compliance and overall user experience quality. According to a study published in Universal Access in the Information Society, web accessibility evaluation from the perception of people with disabilities reveals that the human impact and user experience must be factored into technical accessibility criteria (Springer, 2025). An unmet accessibility criterion can generate several deep emotional aspects. The perception of web accessibility is built through multiple experiences in which people face barriers not only from the interaction with each particular website. This research emphasizes that accessibility is not merely a technical checkbox but a core component of user-centered design that affects all users, positioning accessibility as a fundamental quality attribute rather than a specialized concern.

### Accessibility Education in Computing: Persistent Barriers

Despite growing awareness of digital accessibility importance, significant barriers persist in computing education. Research published in Frontiers in Computer Science reveals that requirements for accessibility are absent from many structured education programs, such as program accreditation, and curriculum and degree requirements (Frontiers, 2024). As a result, many professionals who have access to knowledge and skills are self-taught rather than formally educated or trained. This educational gap has profound implications for the accessibility of digital products and services, as developers who lack formal training in accessibility principles are less likely to proactively consider accessibility in their work. The research underscores the urgent need for educational interventions that integrate accessibility throughout computing curricula rather than treating it as an optional or specialized topic.

### Accessibility in Higher Education: Current State and Challenges

The state of accessibility in online learning environments presents both opportunities and challenges. According to research in Educational Technology Research and Development, online learning has the potential to meet the needs of disabled students in unique ways (Springer, 2024). However, questions remain about how well online learning is meeting the needs of disabled students and in what ways institutions are prepared for and actively providing students with accessible and inclusive online learning opportunities. This research highlights that while technology offers unprecedented opportunities for inclusive education, realizing this potential requires intentional design, adequate resources, and institutional commitment to accessibility as a core value rather than a compliance requirement.

## Related Studies

This section examines prior research on automated evaluation tools and institutional case studies to understand the current landscape and the limitations of existing solutions.

### Coverage of Web Accessibility Guidelines Provided by Automated Checking Tools

Pelzetter et al. (2025) conducted a comprehensive technological evaluation of well-known web accessibility checkers including Pa11y, A11yWatch, and QualWeb against the guidelines of WCAG 2.1. The researchers discovered a crucial limitation: automated tools can reliably flag only 13% of WCAG 2.2 AA success criteria with mostly accurate detection (7 of 55 criteria). These are the technical, measurable criteria where mathematical calculations or binary checks produce consistent results—color contrast ratios, missing page titles, and HTML validation errors. The study found that 42% of criteria (23 of 55) cannot be detected at all by automated means, as these involve subjective evaluation of quality, meaning, and user experience that automated tools cannot assess. The median number of unique violated success criteria detected is only two or less for most tools, and the tools cover only one sixth of all WCAG success criteria. This study concludes that automated tools are not all-inclusive systems but deliver efficient standards for evaluating key technical issues, while human judgment remains essential for comprehensive accessibility assessment.

### Exploring the Relationship Between Web Accessibility and User Experience

An analysis-based discourse study of Aizpurua, Harper, and Vigo (2021) increased the debate around web accessibility from an analytic perspective with conformance and legal orders in sight. The research has a particular type of investigation to determine cause and effect is concerned with investigating the relationship between adherence to Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) and User Experience (UX) standards. The participant performance was measured through factual measures like success time and completion time. The researchers found a solid positive correlation between WCAG compliance and quality of UX beyond many respondents. Crucially, the improvements help the full functionality and inclusivity for all users special for those persons with disabilities. This research provides empirical evidence that accessibility improvements benefit not only users with disabilities but enhance the overall user experience for all users, supporting the business case for accessibility investment.

### Accessibility Insights from Student Software Engineering Projects

Aljedaani et al. (2025) studied real-world evidence of teaching web accessibility through "accessibility sprints" in multiple software engineering courses, then assessed student projects from their activity in a critical manner. Their findings are critical for the foundation of this capstone project: they cataloged the specific, recurring types of accessibility errors students made and identified which WCAG success criteria proved most difficult for learners to implement correctly. This study moves beyond theorizing about educational gaps to documenting them with real student work, demonstrating that even within a structured course, a significant disconnect exists between knowing standards and applying them in practice. The research presented at SIGCSE 2025 underscores a clear need for educational interventions that provide scaffolded, practical feedback tailored to common beginner mistakes. The proposed Web-Based Accessibility Evaluation Tool directly responds to this identified need. It is designed not as a comprehensive audit engine for experts but as a pedagogical instrument that prioritizes clear explanations for high-impact, frequently missed criteria, thereby aiming to bridge the very gap between theory and application that this study so clearly illuminates.

### Teaching Digital Accessibility to Computing Professionals

Research on teaching digital accessibility reveals persistent challenges in both academic and professional settings. A study examining teaching digital accessibility in computing education found that very few CS faculty teach accessibility, and the top barriers they perceive remain consistent across surveys (arXiv, 2024). The barriers include lack of time in already crowded curricula, insufficient instructor knowledge and confidence, and absence of appropriate teaching materials and tools. This research corroborates earlier findings that accessibility education requires not only curriculum integration but also adequate tool support that bridges the gap between complex technical standards and practical implementation skills. The study emphasizes that effective accessibility education requires a combination of theoretical knowledge, hands-on practice, and appropriate tool support that provides immediate feedback and guidance.

### Automated Accessibility Testing: Capabilities and Limitations

Recent research has provided more precise measurements of automated accessibility testing capabilities. According to industry analysis, automated accessibility scanners—including axe DevTools, WAVE, Lighthouse, and Pa11y—catch roughly 30 to 40% of WCAG criteria failures (TestParty, 2024). Manual audits with credentialed auditors catch the remaining 60 to 70%. Neither approach is sufficient alone. Research consistently shows that automated accessibility testing tools detect approximately 30-40% of WCAG violations, with the UK Government Digital Service study finding that automated tools caught only about 30% of issues across their tested sites. This limitation is not due to poor tool design but rather reflects the inherent nature of many accessibility requirements that involve human judgment, context, and user experience considerations that cannot be algorithmically determined. The research emphasizes that automated tools are most valuable when used as part of a comprehensive accessibility strategy that includes manual testing and user evaluation.

## Synthesis

The reviewed literature and related studies together elaborate a comprehensive understanding of challenges and opportunities in web accessibility evaluation. First, Pelzetter et al. (2025) establishes that automated evaluation tools can reliably flag only 13% of WCAG criteria—technical issues such as missing image alt text, color contrast violations, and HTML validation errors—which are suitable for algorithmic assessment but not the entire content quality or logical flows of a web page. The study reveals that 42% of WCAG criteria cannot be detected at all by automated means, as they require subjective evaluation of meaning, context, and user experience.

Aizpurua et al. (2021) focuses on finding the solid relationship between web accessibility and great user experience (UX). They proved that following the concrete WCAG 2.1 guidelines provides more benefits for actual inclusivity of wide-spread users including individuals with impairments. This means accessibility is not just a technical checkbox; it's a core part of good design that improves usability for everyone. The research provides empirical evidence through measured task completion rates and user satisfaction scores, demonstrating that accessibility improvements have quantifiable positive impacts on user experience.

Aljedaani et al. (2025) dissects the educational side of accessibility implementation. Their study, presented at SIGCSE 2025, found that applying accessibility guidelines is quite difficult to learn for students and beginners. One of the key factors is the overly technical way of giving feedback from professional accessibility tools such as WAVE and axe DevTools, which creates difficulty in bridging effective learning and proper application. The research documented specific, recurring accessibility errors in student projects, revealing patterns in which WCAG success criteria prove most challenging for learners to implement correctly.

Recent research on accessibility education reveals systemic barriers. Studies show that very few computing faculty teach accessibility, with top barriers including lack of time in crowded curricula, insufficient instructor knowledge, and absence of appropriate teaching materials (arXiv, 2024). Furthermore, accessibility requirements are absent from many program accreditation standards and curriculum requirements, resulting in professionals who are self-taught rather than formally trained (Frontiers, 2024). This educational gap has direct consequences for the accessibility of digital products and services.

These studies collectively reveal that the complex gap between accessibility standards and widespread implementation exists not just because of limitations in automated evaluation tools, but more fundamentally due to lack of user-friendly educational resources and appropriate pedagogical approaches. Professional tools like axe, WAVE, and Google Lighthouse are powerful but not designed for learning environments, particularly in educational and beginner settings. They produce technical reports optimized for experienced developers rather than explanatory feedback suitable for learners. This creates a crucial barrier in translating knowledge into action.

The persistent implementation gap is further evidenced by large-scale empirical data. The WebAIM Million Report 2025 found that 94.8% of the world's top home pages have detectable WCAG failures, with the same six error categories remaining most prevalent for six consecutive years. This indicates not merely a lack of tools but a systemic failure in education, awareness, and practical skill development. Research shows that automated tools detect only 30-40% of WCAG violations, with the remaining 60-70% requiring manual evaluation and human judgment.

This project, the Development of a Web-Based Accessibility Tool for Inclusive Web Design, isn't trying to duplicate or replace professional tools. Instead, it is designed as a learning aid that bridges the gap between knowing the rules of WCAG 2.1 guidelines and actually applying them to make web design more inclusive. The system addresses the documented educational needs by providing plain-language feedback and suggested adjustments over technical reporting, making accessibility more approachable for students and new developers from the start.

The tool's focus on the 13% of WCAG criteria that can be reliably automated—such as missing alt text, color contrast, heading structure, form labels, and HTML validation—aligns with research findings about what automated tools can effectively detect. By concentrating on these high-impact, objectively testable criteria and providing educational context for each issue, the system addresses the specific learning barriers documented in recent research. The emphasis on plain-language explanations responds directly to findings that students struggle with technical feedback from professional tools and prefer simplified, contextual guidance.

Furthermore, the system's design as a locally deployable, educational platform addresses institutional barriers identified in accessibility education research. By providing a tool specifically designed for academic settings—with features like practice exercises, tutorials, and progressive learning support—the project responds to the documented absence of appropriate teaching materials and tools in computing education. The dual input method (URL and HTML code) supports different learning scenarios, from analyzing existing websites to evaluating student projects during development.
